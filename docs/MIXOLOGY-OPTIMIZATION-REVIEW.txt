# REVIEW 3 — BUILD A BETTER TASTE-LEARNING SYSTEM

This is a major improvement opportunity.

The system must learn more than whether a recipe was approved.

It should develop an increasingly useful understanding of Nick's preferences, Jenny's preferences, and their shared cocktail tastes.

However, collecting that information must remain quick, natural, and optional.

## 3.1 Preference signals

Design a lightweight data collection approach for:

- Spirits and cocktail families enjoyed.
- Ingredients regularly preferred or avoided.
- Sweetness preference.
- Acidity and tartness preference.
- Bitterness preference.
- Spirit-forward versus lighter drinks.
- Texture and mouthfeel.
- Seasonal ingredient preferences.
- Preparation complexity tolerance.
- Ingredient substitutions.
- Repeatability: Would they make this again?
- Flavor combinations they particularly enjoy.
- Changes made after tasting.
- Whether a revised version improved the drink.

Do not require every field to be answered.

## 3.2 Prioritize natural feedback

Support conversational feedback such as:

"Too sweet for me."

"Jenny likes this one, but I think the cranberry needs to come through more."

"The club soda version was better than the Sprite version."

"I'd definitely make this one again."

The system should preserve the original comments and extract possible preference signals.

Do not turn speculative interpretations into permanent preferences without appropriate confirmation.

Provide a simple way for either host to correct an inaccurate preference.

## 3.3 Evidence hierarchy

Treat feedback signals differently according to reliability.

Proposed hierarchy:

1. Actual tasting and explicit repeat-worthiness feedback.
2. Structured feedback on a tested recipe.
3. Explicit statements of preferences.
4. Repeated intentional ingredient changes.
5. Previously approved recipes.
6. Untested saved concepts.

Trends, popularity, and online engagement must never be treated as evidence that Nick or Jenny enjoyed a cocktail.

Do not infer long-term preferences from one isolated tasting.

## 3.4 Independent and shared preferences

Maintain distinct taste histories for Nick and Jenny.

Do not simply average their preferences into one indistinct score.

Identify meaningful areas of agreement and disagreement where supported by sufficient evidence.

The system should be able to recommend an alternative that accommodates both people, or explain why two recipe variations might work better.

## 3.5 Explainable recommendations

When the system makes a recommendation based on taste history, it should be capable of explaining why.

For example:

"Based on your feedback on three previously tested drinks, you generally preferred the less-sweet variations."

Only make such claims when the underlying records support them.

Show supporting recipes or notes on demand.

Consider recency, sample size, conflicting feedback, and uncertainty.

Provide ways to view, correct, and delete learned preferences.

Do not create opaque personality scores or meaningless numerical flavor rankings.

## 3.6 Minimal interaction requirement

Propose a tasting interaction that can be completed in seconds.

For example:

- Would make again: Yes / Maybe / No.
- Quick feedback: Too sweet / Too tart / Too bitter / Too strong / Just right.
- Optional personal comments.

These are examples, not mandatory interface requirements.

The system must still support more detailed analysis for users who want it.

**Deliverable:** A proposed preference-learning data model, feedback workflow, evidence rules, and example recommendations.

# REVIEW 4 — RECONSIDER HOW DATA IS VISUALIZED

Determine which collected information would be more useful as a graphic, comparison, timeline, or visual summary.

Do not create graphs merely for decoration.

Visualizations should reduce interpretation time or reveal information that is difficult to identify in plain text.

Evaluate at least the following concepts.

## 4.1 Individual and shared taste profiles

Investigate side-by-side visual comparisons of Nick's and Jenny's taste preferences.

Potential dimensions include:

- Sweetness.
- Acidity.
- Bitterness.
- Cocktail strength.
- Flavor families.
- Preferred spirits.
- Preferred preparation effort.

Use clear labels, supporting sample sizes, and distinctions between measured feedback and inferred tendencies.

Do not imply statistical certainty where insufficient data exists.

## 4.2 Cocktail flavor profiles

Investigate a compact flavor visualization for individual recipes.

Possible methods:

- Horizontal flavor bars.
- Ingredient/flavor tags.
- Small flavor maps.
- Simple comparison charts.

Evaluate whether a radar chart genuinely improves comprehension or merely looks attractive.

Clearly distinguish estimated flavor characteristics from actual tasting feedback.

## 4.3 Recipe version comparison

Design an intuitive visual comparison showing:

- Added ingredients.
- Removed ingredients.
- Changed quantities.
- Modified preparation.
- Tasting improvements or regressions.
- Different feedback from each host.

Prioritize instantly recognizing what changed.

## 4.4 Cocktail library intelligence

Investigate useful overviews such as:

- Cocktail families used across episodes.
- Ingredient repetition.
- Spirit distribution.
- Seasonal combinations.
- Favorite flavor pairings.
- Tested versus untested recipes.

Design these to improve future recipe suggestions.

Do not build an elaborate statistics dashboard merely to display low-volume data.

## 4.5 Production preparation

Investigate a compact timeline showing:

- Shopping needs.
- Make-ahead work.
- Waiting time.
- Day-of assembly.
- Recording readiness.

Display this inside the existing episode workflow.

## 4.6 Research and originality

Consider a compact comparison view of existing cocktails and the Cheese Louise variation.

Emphasize meaningful recipe differences, sources, and research confidence.

Do not present arbitrary originality percentages as factual measurements.

## 4.7 Visualization requirements

For each recommended visualization, specify:

- Information displayed.
- Source of the data.
- Whether values are observed, inferred, or estimated.
- Minimum data needed to make the display useful.
- How uncertainty or missing information is represented.
- Appropriate chart or visual structure.
- Desktop and mobile presentation.
- Accessibility and text alternatives.
- Whether it belongs in the main workspace or a contextual panel.

**Deliverable:** A ranked visualization plan and annotated low-fidelity mockups for the most useful views.

# REVIEW 6 — IMPROVE THE UNDERLYING DATA MODEL

Critically examine the proposed recipe entities and relationships.

Identify opportunities to simplify the schema while preserving reliable versioning and extensibility.

Pay attention to:

- Canonical recipe records.
- Alcoholic and nonalcoholic counterparts.
- Ingredient normalization and measurement units.
- Recipe components such as syrups or macerations.
- Tasting feedback tied to specific versions.
- Actual tested substitutions versus hypothetical suggestions.
- Individual and shared taste preferences.
- Research findings and provenance.
- Movie and episode relationships.
- Recipe duplication detection.
- Approval and publication states.
- Uploaded photographs.
- Listener submissions.
- Source-of-truth ownership.

Ensure ingredient comparisons recognize common alternative names and equivalent units without incorrectly equating different products.

Design reliable data provenance so the system can distinguish:

- Recipe suggestions.
- AI-generated estimates.
- External research.
- User-entered observations.
- Actual tasted versions.
- Approved recipes.
- Published content.

Determine what needs to be stored permanently versus what can be computed from existing records.

Do not duplicate information unnecessarily.

Avoid collecting unrelated personal data or retaining information without a clear product purpose.

**Deliverable:** Recommended logical data model, relationship diagram, data flow, and data retention considerations.

# REVIEW 7 — CHECK LEARNING QUALITY, NOT JUST DATA COLLECTION

Evaluate whether the proposed system could genuinely improve recommendations over time.

Develop test scenarios including:

**Scenario 1:** Nick repeatedly prefers less-sweet cocktails, while Jenny prefers sweeter versions.

**Scenario 2:** Both hosts enjoy a cranberry cocktail but dislike its maple-heavy revision.

**Scenario 3:** A cocktail receives excellent taste feedback but takes too much preparation time.

**Scenario 4:** A frequently suggested ingredient appears in many rejected recipes.

**Scenario 5:** A new cocktail is similar to several previously approved recipes.

**Scenario 6:** Only one prior tasting exists for a particular cocktail family.

For each scenario, describe what the system should learn, what it should not infer, and how future suggestions should change.

Do not recommend opaque machine-learning infrastructure if simpler structured preference rules and well-designed retrieval can achieve the required outcome.

Prefer the least complex reliable approach.

Include tests for incorrect inferences, sparse data, contradictory evidence, and preference corrections.
